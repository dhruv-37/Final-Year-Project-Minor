# Measure new frauds before chasing AUC

The most valuable next step for this project is an evaluation, not a new model. Split the 2003-2012 test frauds by **when their AAER case started**: before the training cutoff (continuing cases), in the gap years, or in the test year itself. Then report AUC, Sens@5% and Prec@1% for each group, and show where the Temporal Transformer and market-feature gains come from. This needs no new data and no retraining. Every per-row score is already in `results/test_predictions.csv`, and the case years are in the paper's own AAER file. It takes about one day. The case for it rests on four facts. First, only 2 of about 40 ideas were ever adopted, each worth +0.005 to +0.008 test AUC, and neither survives a Holm correction. Second, the 2003-2012 test years are spent: every protocol after the first is labelled exploratory. Third, 0.748 already sits at the top of the 0.70-0.75 band that strict out-of-time studies report. Fourth, an independent replication finds that **54% of test-year frauds continue cases that began in the training window, and only 1-3 of 121 genuinely new frauds reach the top 1%** ([ZQ-77777 replication](https://github.com/ZQ-77777/Bao2020-Fraud-Detection-Replication)). The project's own "first-time fraud" audit does not answer this question, because it counts masked continuing cases as first-time. If a model change is wanted as well, the most gate-ready one is a balanced-context TabPFN-2.5 ensemble member on a free GPU. Its realistic expected test gain is 0 to +0.003 AUC, with perhaps a one-in-six chance of passing the +0.002 validation gate. The idea with the largest potential is to replace the blunt 2-year gap with labels "as known at the test date". That is first an evaluation change, and only then a model change.

## The project has reached the strict-evaluation ceiling

The literature numbers are only comparable when the split type is clear. Under the full Bao rules (train to t-2, serial-fraud recoding), corrected RUSBoost scores **0.7228 for 2003-2008 and 0.7196 for 2003-2014**, and the 14-ratio logit scores 0.6711 ([Bao et al. 2021 reply](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1184/BaoEtAlMar2021.pdf)). Walker's re-runs move only between 0.735 and 0.750 across tree counts, so tuning and seeds alone shift AUC by about ±0.005-0.01 ([Walker 2022](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1245/WalkerSept2022.pdf)). The project's 0.748 on 2003-2012 is at or above every corrected figure on the Bao dataset. Its own leak diagnostics match outside evidence: removing serial masking lifts the same model from 0.741 to 0.788, and also removing the gap lifts it to 0.828 ([README](../README.md)). An independent Python replication moves from 0.715 to 0.778 when the serial fix is removed ([Teymur102](https://github.com/Teymur102/accounting-fraud-detection)).

CI-FSFD shows how much the split type matters. Under random 5-fold CV, its flexible models gain 0.20-0.30 AUC over company-isolated CV, while a 7-variable logit gains almost nothing (0.68 vs 0.67) ([CI-FSFD](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19259v1)). So any AAER result above about 0.85 should be presumed to come from leakage. Two further claims cannot be placed in this table. Brown, Crowley and Elliott's topic-model gain ("up to 59%") is a relative detection gain on an out-of-sample design, not AUC points ([Brown et al. 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v58y2020i1p237-291.html)). BERT-on-MD&A results (0.844 / 0.661 / 0.797 across variants) come from a search summary whose split was not verified ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392594631_Artificial_Intelligence_in_fraud_detection_textual_analysis_of_10-K_filings)).

The project's own history sets a hard bar. The validation-to-test shrinkage is severe. The Temporal Transformer kept 67% of its CV gain (+0.0126 → +0.0084). The market + recency + retune combination kept only 35% (+0.0145 → +0.0051) ([README](../README.md); [PROTOCOL_AUC](../experiments/PROTOCOL_AUC.md)). The paired test-year CI for a gain is about ±0.005, so a new idea needs roughly +0.006 test AUC to be significant. On past shrinkage, that means a fresh-seed CV gain of about +0.009 to +0.017, which only those two adopted changes ever reached. The fresh 2015-2017 period cannot rescue a small gain either. It has 55 frauds against 358 in 2003-2012. Scaling the paired CI by the square root of that ratio is a rough estimate, but it gives about ±0.013, which is far wider than any plausible single-idea gain ([PROTOCOL_FRESH](../experiments/PROTOCOL_FRESH.md)).

## Top recommendation: split test frauds by case start

### What to build
For each test year T, use the case's first fraud year *s* to put every test-year fraud firm-year into one of three groups. "Continuing" means *s* ≤ T-2: the case has years inside the training window, and those were masked to 0. "Gap-start" means *s* = T-1. "New" means *s* = T. A fourth flag can mark recidivist firms, whose firm has an earlier, different case. Then compute these numbers for every model column in `results/test_predictions.csv`: yearly AUC, Sens@5% and Prec@1%/top-1% hits, with each group's frauds scored against all non-frauds of that year. The columns cover the baseline RUSBoost, LGBM/XGB/LR, the TT alone, the ensemble without firm history, the previous ensemble and the proposed model. Use the project's existing paired bootstrap, clustered by case and firm. Then repeat the same split on the 3-seed cached scores, to decompose the two adopted gains (+TT, and +market/recency/retune) by group. The same can be done on the fresh 2015-2017 predictions.

### Why this question is open
The existing audit removes only frauds "whose firm has a fraud label left in training" after masking. That leaves 231 of 240 validation frauds. Masked continuing cases count as "first-time" under that rule, so the reported 0.769 → 0.784 says nothing about persistence ([audit_auc.py](../experiments/audit_auc.py); [audit_auc.json](../results/experiments/audit_auc.json)). Outside evidence says the split matters. Walker found that 10 of the 16 published top-1% hits came from 17 AAER cases whose training years had escaped recoding ([Walker 2021](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1185/WalkerMar2021.pdf)). Bao et al. report that about **20%** of test-year frauds in 2003-2005 are recoded in training, falling to 6.12% in 2006 and 3.74% in 2007 ([Bao et al. 2021](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1184/BaoEtAlMar2021.pdf)). The ZQ replication puts the continuing share at **54%** ([ZQ-77777](https://github.com/ZQ-77777/Bao2020-Fraud-Detection-Replication)). The two figures disagree, probably because they define the groups differently, and only the project's own count settles it for this data. The Temporal Transformer is the component most exposed to persistence. Masking hides the labels of a continuing case's earlier years, but the TT still reads those years' features. Market features carry a separate risk: the README already warns that a fall in market value may follow disclosure ([README](../README.md)). The direction of the effect is not obvious in advance. Masking trains the trees to call a continuing case's earlier years clean, which could push those firms *down*. That uncertainty is what makes the measurement worth a section of the final report.

### Data, evaluation and expected result
Everything is on disk. You need the per-row scores in `results/test_predictions.csv`, the `p_aaer` case IDs in the paper file, and `data/external/fresh/jarfraud/AAER_firm_year.csv`. Take case years from that file rather than from the Compustat rows. Firm-years dropped from the sample are exactly what split cases in the Walker bug, and the AAER file still lists them ([Walker 2021](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1185/WalkerMar2021.pdf)). Write a short `PROTOCOL_CASES.md` before computing anything, as Protocols 7 and 8 did. It should fix the group definitions, the metrics, the minimum number of frauds per group-year (pool years where a group is thin), and the readings that would count as "gain survives on new cases". No validation gate applies, because no model changes. The test years are reused only descriptively, and nothing is linked through EDGAR, so the linkage guard does not apply. The output is not an AUC gain. It is a credible answer to the examiner's obvious question: "does your model detect new fraud, or recognise ongoing fraud?" The ZQ evidence suggests the top-1% hits will be dominated by continuing cases, and that new-case AUC will sit below the all-fraud figure. If the TT gain holds on new cases, the project's main contribution becomes much stronger. If it does not, reporting that honestly is still the right result.

### Risks and effort
New-case counts per year will be small. With 358 test frauds and a 20-54% continuing share, roughly 165-285 frauds are non-continuing, which is about 15-30 a year. So report pooled results and CIs, not single-year AUCs. The group definitions need one sensitivity check: case start taken from the AAER file versus from the first labelled row. Effort is about 0.5-1.5 days, including the protocol, one results table and one slide.

The as-known-labels idea ranks first among the alternatives because it attacks the constraint that every source names: the labels. In Zavitsanos et al., only about **50% / 70% / 80%** of positives one, two and three years back were already known at a 2003 test date ([Zavitsanos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17457)). The project's training labels use hindsight (AAERs up to 2018), so the 2-year gap is a fixed stand-in for delay. A side effect is that **delay-aware down-weighting of recent negatives cannot be shown to help under the current protocol**: those negatives are not actually contaminated in the hindsight labels. It only becomes testable once realistic labels exist. Bao et al. themselves argue that reveal-date handling is the principled alternative to recoding by case ([Bao et al. 2021](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1184/BaoEtAlMar2021.pdf)). Recency weighting was one of the few things that helped here (+0.0059 fresh CV), which hints that recovering year T-1 is worth something. But Zavitsanos' linear models gained nothing from gap changes.

TabPFN is the most protocol-ready pure model idea. It uses the same features, fits the balanced-bag design, and is evaluable on the 1999-2001 folds. Balanced or hybrid context sampling adds 3-4 AUC points over uniform sampling in credit data ([Tanna et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.18635)). But every financial comparison found has TabPFN losing to, or at best matching, GBDTs, and none used a temporal split. The project's history adds a stronger warning. Everything that helped brought new information (firm history, market movement) or a better use of time (recency weights). No learner on the same single-year features has ever helped.

## Model gains and evaluation gains answer different questions

The evidence supports saying it plainly: **for a final-year project, the next evaluation step is worth more than the next model step.** A model idea that passes the gate is expected to add +0.001 to +0.005 test AUC. That cannot be confirmed as significant on the spent 2003-2012 years after Holm correction, nor on the 55-fraud fresh period. Its realistic outcome is another "promising, not confirmed" row. The case-start split instead produces a result that is informative whichever way it comes out. It also targets the weakness that sank the original paper: Walker's critique and the JAR erratum were about continuing cases inflating top-1% hits ([Walker 2021](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1185/WalkerMar2021.pdf); [Walker 2022](https://econjwatch.org/File+download/1245/WalkerSept2022.pdf)). The project already reports that AUC and top-of-list metrics disagree: the latest gain lowered Prec@1% from 2.1% to 1.9% ([README](../README.md)). That makes the per-group top-k breakdown especially relevant.

Two lower-cost evaluation additions belong in the same results section. One is within-size AUC (alternative 2). The other is a per-year DeLong or bootstrap CI table, since report item 12.6 already lists "stronger statistics" as unfinished. Both reuse saved predictions. If time remains after the case-start split, the as-known-labels regime is the evaluation upgrade with the most literature behind it. It also tests whether the deployable model's claim would have held in real time.

## Ideas to drop, and why

The modelling literature review's own top suggestion, a RUSBoost member with temporally fitted ensemble weights, **was already tested**. Adding the enhanced RUSBoost to LGBM+XGB+LR lowered CV AUC from 0.7603 to 0.7569 ([ensemble_selection.csv](../results/ensemble_selection.csv)). Unequal blend weights (A7) were tested too: the TT×3 version passed CV alone (+0.0042 fresh), but its test-year combination was rejected (+0.0043, p 0.095) ([README](../README.md)).

## Conclusion

The project's selection process can no longer pick out a winning model. With three validation years, a spent test period and a 55-fraud holdout, any model gain small enough to be realistic is also too small to confirm. So the strongest next contribution is to sharpen what the 0.748 means: how much of it comes from new frauds rather than ongoing ones, from firm size, and from labels that a real user would not yet have. Those questions are cheap, use data already on disk, and give a decisive answer whatever the result. They also turn the project's biggest exposure, the same continuing-case effect that undid the original paper's top-1% claim, into a measured finding.

Several things remain unknown. This review did not count the project's actual continuing-case share. It did not check that the AAER manifest carries release dates. And no study has put TabPFN or MD&A content through a gap-and-masking protocol. If the case-start split shows the Temporal Transformer's gain surviving on new cases, the firm-history result becomes the project's headline. If it does not, the honest framing is that the model is good at recognising ongoing fraud, and the as-known-labels regime becomes the natural next experiment.
